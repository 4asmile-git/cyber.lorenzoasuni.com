Three questions I get asked about this market.

I never asked any of them. I was busy at the booth.

Why is security becoming the base layer of every technology?

Every product is now software, connected and, more and more, run by AI agents. Each one needs identity, access rules, logs and proof of compliance before a customer can buy it.

European rules made that a condition of sale. NIS2 pushes obligations down the supply chain, DORA does it for finance, the Cyber Resilience Act for any product with digital parts.

Every tech company now sells security, or has to pass through it to sell anything.

Why has it changed so much in a few years?

Three moves at once. The perimeter went to the cloud and to people's homes, so the old products stopped fitting. Ransomware and regulation took the subject to the board and to the insurer. And capital funded a new category, with a new acronym, almost every year.

Two things are reshaping it again: platforms are absorbing single products, and AI agents now do the first read of an alert.

There are not enough security people, which is why managed services keep growing.

Why is it so slow to change how it sells?

Founders are engineers who sold to engineers, and for years that was enough. The purchase runs on trust, careful buyers reward the familiar, so everyone copies the market leader.

Much of the revenue passes through partners, and many vendors have never spoken to the people who use their product. Customers rarely agree to be named. A six-month cycle hides what works until the budget has gone to the same events as last year.

The slowness is the opening for a company that explains itself plainly and measures its channels.

Lead generation has a ceiling. Communication moves it.

I used to put the whole budget on events. Events have snacks.

In any quarter only a small share of your market is buying. Lead generation decides how many of those accounts you reach. Communication decides how many of them answer, because buyers reply to names they already know. Nobody decides how many are in market.

What the vendor needs from the partner

  • Accounts it cannot reach alone.
  • People who can install and run the product without a call to support.
  • A forecast it can believe.

What the partner needs from the vendor

  • Leads it is allowed to keep: a deal registration that holds.
  • Funds that come with a plan, and a person to run it with.
  • A product its own service can be built on.
  • A reason to put this vendor first among ten.

Five things to agree before the first joint campaign

  1. Who owns a lead, and for how many days.
  2. Whose name is on the proposal.
  3. What the partner must be able to do without the vendor in the room.
  4. What the funds pay for, with a target.
  5. Which four numbers both sides read every quarter.

Why the two need each other, and how to speed it up.

Alone I am a fossil. With a partner I am a sales team.

The vendor has the product and the marketing budget. The partner has the customer and the customer's trust. A mid-size buyer wants one name that answers for the tool and for the people who run it. Neither side can offer that alone: the vendor cannot afford to sell to and support thousands of mid-size accounts, and the partner cannot build the product.

Left alone, the relationship follows a script. A contract is signed, a logo goes on two websites, a few leads are passed and never followed, the marketing funds expire unspent. Two to three quarters go by before the partner closes a deal without the vendor in the room. Working together on purpose shortens that. This is the quarter I run.

Who sells to whom, and where I have worked.

A security product reaches its buyer through three or four companies. Each one has a different commercial problem. I have worked inside two of them.

Vendor

Builds the product. Usually has a strong technical story and a category where every competitor makes the same claim. Needs pipeline it can attribute, and partners who sell rather than partners who sign and wait.

    Field notes.

    Eight rules. I broke all of them before lunch, most years.

    Next quarter is already in the CRM.

    With a six-month cycle, an opportunity opened today closes two quarters from now. To move next quarter, work the pipeline you have.

    Count closers before you hire SDRs.

    Account executives multiplied by the opportunities each can work in a month is the ceiling. Meetings above it lower the win rate.

    Give every event a target, a full cost and an owner.

    Follow-up starts within 24 hours and its owner is chosen before the event.

    In Germany, start with the phone.

    Cold email to businesses generally needs prior consent there, so calls, LinkedIn and events carry more of the work. Check with counsel before buying tools.

    Read your claim on a competitor's homepage.

    If it still fits, it describes the category. Say what you detect, how fast, and for whom.

    Ask for vendor funds with a plan.

    Audience, date, expected meetings. A request with numbers is easier to approve than a request for budget.

    Put vendor and partner in the same room for a day.

    One shared list, the vendor's people next to the partner's callers. It produces meetings and it trains the team.

    Write for the person who has to explain it upstairs.

    Compliance deadlines put security on the agenda of mid-size companies. A plain explanation of the obligation opens more doors than a threat report.
